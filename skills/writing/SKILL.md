# Universal Rules (all formats)

## Voice and Tone

- **Clear and direct.** Say what you mean. No filler, no decoration.
- **Authoritative.** You are the expert. Never hedge with "it is said that," "possibly," "probably," "studies show" (cite them instead), or "is meant to."
- **Write like you speak.** But don't be cheesy. Avoid cliches and obscure metaphors.
- **Active voice.** 90%+ of the time. Passive voice makes writing ambiguous and hard to follow.
- **Gender-neutral language.** Use "they" instead of "he" or "she."
- **First mention of real people.** On the first mention of any real named person, put a 1–2 word descriptor immediately before the name: *mathematician Claude Shannon*, *business professor Sophie Leroy*, *psychologist Theresa Glomb*. Later mentions use the name alone. Zhenia and the reader are never named in the draft; this rule does not apply to them.

## Readability (Hemingway Standard)

Target a 5th-6th grade reading level. Every rule below keeps text scannable and easy to read on a phone screen.

### Paragraphs
- **2-3 sentences max.** If a paragraph hits 4 sentences, split it. Most paragraphs should be 2 sentences. (SOPs may run longer; see below.)
- One idea per paragraph. If the topic shifts, start a new paragraph.
- White space is a feature, not a waste.

### Sentences
- Target ~20 words per sentence. Hard ceiling of 25 words. If a sentence runs longer, break it in two.
- Vary sentence length deliberately. Short sentences punch. Medium sentences carry the argument forward. Longer sentences build momentum when the reader is rested. Mix all three. Don't just write words. Write music.
- No more than 3 consecutive sentences of similar length.
- Limit subordinate clauses. One per sentence at most. After a sentence with a comma-separated clause, make the next one short and direct.
- No compressed signposts. A sentence that leans on two ideas the reader has not yet been given ("let me have it both ways", "comes last because it broke first") reads as a riddle on first hearing. Name the two sides, or state the reason, in the same sentence or the next one. Test: someone hearing it once, with no diagram and no second read, can say what it means. If they cannot, unpack it or cut it.
  - Before: "How those notes become memory the agents can actually use is the last part of this video, and that's deliberate: memory was the first thing that broke, so it has the most to teach."
  - After: "The last part of this video is about how those notes become memory the agents can actually use, and that's deliberate, because memory was the first thing that broke, so it has the most to teach."
  - Takeaway: Lead with the subject ("the last part of this video is about…"), never with a long noun clause ("how those notes become memory… comes last").
- Single spaces between sentences.

### Word Choice
- Use the simplest word that carries the meaning. "Use" not "utilize." "Start" not "commence." "About" not "approximately." "Help" not "facilitate." "Show" not "demonstrate."
- Avoid words with 4+ syllables when a shorter word works.
- Cut empty adverbs: "very," "really," "extremely," "highly," "actually," "basically." If the adverb does not change the meaning, delete it.
- Prefer concrete nouns over abstract ones.

### Scanability
- Front-load each paragraph with the most useful information.
- Use bold sparingly for key terms or takeaways. Do not bold entire sentences.
- Use bullet points for lists of 3+ items. Do not force lists where prose reads better.
- H2 and H3 headings should be scannable summaries of the section content.

## Formatting Basics

- Use **bullet points and tables** to present information clearly.
- Use **bolding** for emphasis, but sparingly.
- Spell out acronyms on first use.
- Use hyperlinks inline when referencing specific information or resources.
- No em dashes. No semicolons.

## Banned AI Patterns

AI-generated text follows predictable patterns. Avoid all of the following to keep writing human and natural.

### Banned Phrases
- "In conclusion" / "In summary"
- "Not only X, but also Y"
- "It's not just X. It's also Y." / "Not just..." / "Don't just..."
- Any sentence that explains something then pivots with a "not..." contrast
- "It is important to note that"
- "That being said"
- "Ultimately"
- "Furthermore" / "Moreover"
- "Additionally" (as a sentence opener)
- "For instance" / "For example" (when overused)
- "This means that"
- "In other words"
- "As previously mentioned"
- "Crucial role" / "Play a crucial role" / "Vital role"
- "Whether you're a [X] or a [Y]" / "Whether..."
- "Where most..."
- "Here's the part nobody talks about..."
- "Here's the kicker."
- "But here's the thing."
- "Then I realized..."
- "The result?"
- "Hot take."
- "In plain English." / "In simple terms." / "In practical terms."
- "It's simple." / "Is simple." / "Is clear."
- "That matters." / "It matters."
- "Pay attention."
- "No fluff."
- "No [X]. No [Y]. Just [Z]."
- "From X to Y: [Title]."
- "In today's fast-paced digital landscape."
- "In the dynamic world of..."
- "As the world continues to evolve..."
- "Looming challenges."
- "Advantages and disadvantages" when it feels overly diplomatic or bland
- "Dive into" / "Unlock" / "Unleash"
- "Price of admission"

### Banned Overused Words
Find more natural, specific alternatives for these AI favorites:

- "Significant" / "Crucial" / "Essential" / "Important" (when used generically for emphasis)
- "Beneficial"
- "Solution" (prefer "approach," "method," or something concrete)
- "Impactful"
- "Enhance"
- "Efficient" / "Increased efficiency"
- "Optimize"
- "Challenges" (when used generically for "problems")
- "Lucrative"
- "Ensures"
- "Quietly"
- "Infrastructure" (unless literally about infrastructure)
- "Narrative"
- "Rails" (unless literally about Rails/railways)
- "Noise" / "Noisy" (unless literal)
- "Zoom" / "Zooming" / "Zooming out" (unless about the app)
- "Cooled"

### Banned Structural Patterns
- **Paired word endings:** Don't end sentences with two words joined by "and" (e.g., "safety and efficiency," "quality and reliability"). Break the pattern.
- **Formulaic transitions:** Don't chain paragraphs with "Additionally," "Furthermore," "Moreover." Use organic transitions or none at all.
- **Rule-of-three / triplets:** "X, Y, and Z" when it feels like padding.
- **Sudden structured lists or bullets** when they feel unnatural in context.
- **Overly neat contrast framing** that reads like a school essay.
- **Generic "balanced" framing** that sounds diplomatic but says nothing.
- **Random emojis** inserted mid-sentence.
- **Cliche intros** that sound polished but empty.
- **Grand "modern world" openings.**
- **LinkedIn-with-caffeine-and-a-thesaurus tone.**
- **No two-part summary sentences.** A sentence built as a maxim — 'A [thing] that does X is the one that Y', 'The system that X is the system that Y', a subject clause that sets up a verdict clause — reads as mechanical. Worst as the second sentence of a comment, where it sums up the first. Say the specific observation instead (what happened, to whom, when), or cut the line. Example to avoid: 'A system that asks for upkeep before it gives anything back is the one that sits empty two weeks later.'

# Format: Articles and Blog Posts

Apply these **in addition to** universal rules when writing articles, blog posts, or educational content.

## Tone
- **Conversational, not academic.** Write like you're teaching a class, not reading a textbook. Teach, don't tell.
- **Avoid first-person** ("I," "me," "myself") unless the piece is explicitly personal. Use "we" or rephrase.

## Structure
- **Don't bury the lead.** The lead (the answer to the title's promise) goes in the opening section. Keep intros under 100 words. Use the Problem-Agitate-Solution (PAS) formula to hook the reader. The first paragraph opens by answering as many of the 28 questions in `CRAFT.md` (The opening) as it can.
- **The slippery slope.** Every sentence should make the reader want to read the next one. Ask questions, then answer them. Take the reader on a journey.
- **Articles are stories** with a beginning, middle, and end. Write an "educational story," not a research paper.
- **Make it visual.** Create images in the reader's mind.
- For longer pieces (800+ words), include a brief bullet-point summary after the introduction.
- Conclusions should include 1-2 key takeaways.

## Quality Checklist (Articles)

1. Lead is not buried.
2. Readability is 6th-8th grade level.
3. No AI-banned phrases or words.
4. Sentence length varies.
5. Paragraphs are 2-3 sentences.
6. Active voice throughout.
7. Acronyms spelled out on first use.
8. Facts are cited with sources.
9. The piece reads like a story, not a research paper.

## Article Template (full blog articles)

When the task is a full article, follow the workflow and template below as well. The template is brand-agnostic; apply the target brand's styling, linking, and source requirements on top.

### Research Workflow

Every article follows this sequence. Do not skip steps.

**Step 1: Deep Research**
- Web search the primary topic, related subtopics, and any technical or factual claims. Use multiple searches.
- Verify every factual claim against primary sources. Never rely on training data alone.
- Check for recent changes to regulations, standards, or industry data.

**Step 2: SEO Keyword Research**
- Search for the primary keyword. Check what terms competitors rank for.
- Pick a primary keyword (goes in title, H1, meta description, intro, conclusion).
- Pick 3-5 secondary keywords.
- Check People Also Asked. Pull 4-6 PAA questions.
- Match search intent from top-ranking pages.
- Map keywords to sections before writing.

**Step 3: Internal Link Research**
- Search the target site for related existing content using `site:[domain] [topic]`.
- Aim for 3-6 internal links per article, distributed naturally.

**Step 4: External Source Linking**
- Link directly to source when referencing data, regulations, or official resources.
- Use SEO-friendly anchor text. Weave source links into copy.
- Prioritize .gov, .edu, and official organization domains.
- Every article should have 2-4 external source links.

### Article Structure

1. **Metadata Block** (slug, meta description 140-160 chars, category, PAA questions, notes)
2. **SEO Targeting Strategy** (primary keyword, secondary keywords, search intent, keyword placement map, internal/external linking targets)
3. **Featured Image** (italicized placeholder)
4. **Title (H1)** — must include primary keyword, under 60 chars, add year tag for evergreen content
5. **Introduction** — 3-5 sentences, hook + bridge + CTA, under 100 words
6. **Key Takeaways (H2)** — 4 bullet points, single sentences, short and actionable
7. **Video Placeholder** (if relevant video exists)
8. **Body Sections (H2/H3)** — 5-8 H2 sections, 150-300 words each, search-friendly headings
9. **FAQ (H2)** — 4 questions as bold bullet points, 2-3 sentence answers, target PAA queries
10. **Conclusion (H2)** — callback to intro hook, one takeaway, punchy closer, 2-3 sentences

### Quality Checklist (full articles)

- No banned phrases, words, or structural patterns
- Active voice 90%+, acronyms spelled out on first use
- All factual claims verified via web search
- No paragraph exceeds 3 sentences, no sentence exceeds 25 words
- Primary keyword in H1, meta description, intro, and conclusion
- 3-6 internal links and 2-4 external links, all clickable
- Total word count under 3,000 unless specified otherwise

# Format: SOPs and Documentation

Apply these **in addition to** universal rules when writing SOPs, process docs, runbooks, or technical documentation. These rules **override** any conflicting article/blog rules.

## Tone
- **Professional and precise**, not conversational. No rhetorical questions, no "journeys," no storytelling hooks.
- **Neutral third-person.** No "we," "you," or "I." Use role names ("the operator," "the reviewer") or imperative mood ("Run the script," "Verify the output").
- **Instructional, not persuasive.** The reader already needs to do this. Don't sell them on it.

## Structure
- **Follow the template.** SOPs have a fixed structure (Purpose, Scope, Roles, Procedure, Checklist, Version History). Respect it. Don't invent narrative arcs.
- **Lead with the action.** Each step starts with a verb. "Open the dashboard," not "The dashboard should be opened."
- **One action per step.** If a step has "and" in it, split it.
- **Number procedural steps.** Use numbered lists for sequential actions, bullets for non-sequential items.
- **Paragraphs can be longer** than article paragraphs when describing context or scope (3-5 sentences is fine).

## What NOT to Do in SOPs
- No PAS formula or hook-based intros. The Purpose section states what this SOP covers and why. That's it.
- No rhetorical questions ("Why does this matter?").
- No slippery-slope engagement tactics.
- No visual storytelling or metaphors.
- No summary bullet points after the intro (the structure itself is the summary).
- No conclusions with "key takeaways." The checklist serves that role.

## Quality Checklist (SOPs)

1. Every procedure step starts with an imperative verb.
2. One action per step.
3. No AI-banned phrases or words.
4. Active voice throughout.
5. Acronyms spelled out on first use.
6. Roles are named consistently (same term every time).
7. No conversational or persuasive language.
8. Template structure is followed exactly.
